Should I block AI crawlers? robots.txt for NZ businesses

“AI crawler” sounds like one thing. It’s at least three kinds of bot, run by each AI company, and blocking the wrong one can take your business out of ChatGPT, Claude or Perplexity answers. Here’s which is which, and what most NZ businesses should do.

By Eko Media · Reviewed by Freddie Berning, founder · Published 17 November 2026

The short answer

For most NZ businesses that want customers, don’t block them all. Training bots (GPTBot, ClaudeBot, Google-Extended) collect content to build future models, and blocking them is a fair choice. Search bots (OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot, PerplexityBot) are how AI assistants find and cite you, so blocking them takes you out of those answers. User fetchers (ChatGPT-User, Claude-User, Perplexity-User) open a page when someone asks, and some don’t follow robots.txt at all.

Are all AI crawlers the same?

No. Each company runs separate bots for separate jobs, and each one reads its own line in your robots.txt. This is what the companies’ own documentation says each one does (checked 17 Nov 2026).

So should a New Zealand business block AI crawlers?

If you want people to find you through AI, allow the search bots and the user fetchers. They’re what let ChatGPT, Claude and Perplexity read your services, areas and hours, and name you in an answer. A search bot that’s blocked can’t cite you.

Training bots are a real choice. Publishers, paid-content sites and businesses with original research often block them to keep their work out of future models. Many service businesses allow them too, on the view that a model can only mention what it has seen. That’s a judgment call, not a proven win, so decide it on purpose rather than by accident.

  • Want to be found in AI answers? Allow OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot, PerplexityBot and the user fetchers.
  • Don’t want to train models? Disallow GPTBot, ClaudeBot and Google-Extended. That doesn’t remove you from Google Search.
  • Have private pages? Put them behind a login. robots.txt is public and only asks bots to stay out.

Could my site be blocking AI without me knowing?

Yes, and it’s common. A theme, an SEO plugin or a developer can add a blanket rule, and some hosts and CDNs now offer a one-click “block AI bots” setting. Cloudflare made blocking AI crawlers the default for new domains in July 2025. The result looks the same either way: AI assistants can’t read the site, so they don’t name the business.

Check it in two minutes

1. Open yourdomain.co.nz/robots.txt in a browser. Look for Disallow: / under any of the bot names above, or under User-agent: *.

2. Check your host or CDN dashboard for a bot or AI-crawler blocking setting.

3. Or run the free AI Readiness Check, which tests whether the main AI crawlers can reach your pages.

A simple file that keeps you in AI search but opts out of training looks like this:

User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /

User-agent: ClaudeBot
Disallow: /

User-agent: Google-Extended
Disallow: /

User-agent: *
Allow: /

Our own site allows every AI crawler, training included, because being read is our whole business.

The honest bit. robots.txt is a request, not a lock. The big AI companies say their crawlers follow it, user-triggered fetchers may not, and badly behaved scrapers ignore it. Allowing the search bots doesn’t guarantee you’ll be named either. It only removes one reason you can’t be.
